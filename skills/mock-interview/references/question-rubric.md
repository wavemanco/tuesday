# Question types, coverage rules, and writing rules

Personas describe how a question arrives; types describe what it's scoring. Tag every question with a type so the candidate can see the pack's coverage at a glance — and so you can see where coverage is thin before delivering.

## The five types

| Tag | Name | What it's really testing | Tends to dominate |
|---|---|---|---|
| **VAL** | Behavioral / values alignment | Whether past behavior demonstrates the company's *published* values, not generic virtue | Dedicated values rounds; cross-functional interviewers |
| **EXP** | Work-experience deep-dive | Whether the CV's claims survive scrutiny; attribution honesty; what the candidate actually did | Probers; anyone who read the CV closely |
| **JD** | Role / JD-driven | Stated responsibilities and qualifications — can the candidate do the listed job, including its gaps | Hiring managers; skeptics |
| **CO** | Company & strategy knowledge | Homework depth: strategy, competition, recent moves; whether the candidate can think at the company's altitude | Executives; strategy-adjacent roles |
| **CASE** | Hypothetical / live problem | Structure and reasoning on a problem from the actual role, delivered live | Hiring managers; case-led loops |

Dual-tagging is normal — list the primary type first and let the rationale explain the call ("JD content, CASE delivery"). A question with no clear type is usually a question that doesn't belong in the pack.

## Coverage rules

These exist because packs fail by omission, and the omissions are predictable:

- **Every hard requirement in the JD gets at least one JD-tagged question, and every other stated requirement gets one or a stated reason it does not.** The JD is the interviewer's scoring sheet; a requirement with no rehearsed answer is a free miss. The ones that go missing are never the headline requirements — they are the mid-list items, a named methodology or a specific tool, that appear once in the JD, get noted in the fit snapshot, and are never converted into a question. Check the finished banks against the JD's own list before delivering, not against your memory of it.
- **Every headline metric on the CV gets a defend-the-number EXP question.** Quantified claims are magnet targets for probers; the rehearsal is precise attribution, in the candidate's own words — how the figure was counted, what it is sensitive to, the assumption they would defend least, and exactly what was theirs. Where no quote states these, the guidance is an answer slot: the pack never supplies a sizing method, a baseline, an attribution model or an assumption, and "I don't know how it was counted" is an honest branch. A figure the candidate gave is exactly where a pack is tempted to explain how it was reached, and an explanation they cannot repeat under a second question is worse than none.
- **Every published company value gets at least one VAL question with a mapped story** when research detects a values-style round — mapped where one of the candidate's quoted sentences carries a story, and a selection scaffold where none does. Slogan familiarity isn't the bar; a specific decision that demonstrates the value is.
- **At least one CO or CASE question grounded in the company's current strategy** when research at L1+ succeeded. These are the differentiator questions most candidates can't touch — and they go generic without research, which is the honest cost of declining the deep tier.
- **The single most predictable objection to this specific candidate gets the deepest rehearsal** — usually a Skeptic or Pressure item born from the fit snapshot's biggest gap. Name it in the pack as the highest-ROI prep, because it is: this question will be asked in some form, and a rehearsed non-defensive answer changes the interview. Deepest means the most follow-up levels, the most exact concession wording and a limit named before it is asked for — never more affirmative content than the candidate's quotes carry. The objection is usually aimed at exactly where the material is thin, which is where a pack is most tempted to thicken it.
- **Every pre-declared deliverable — a POV, presentation, or take-home the recruiter announced — gets its own build section.** Announced deliverables are graded deliverables: thesis options with a mandatory concession, a differentiation strategy, and the requested close (customer benefit, recommendation) if one was named. A pack that rehearses questions but not the assignment fails the loop.
- **When a provided research artifact is equally available to interviewers or rival candidates, differentiate from it — never recite it.** A public report's shortlist is the consensus answer any prepared candidate assembles; bring one consensus item to prove grounding, then one thesis-driven divergence, and where the role's real job includes saying no, the anti-pick. Reciting a shared source scores as homework; departing from it, with reasons, scores as judgment.
- **The pack carries a requirement-coverage line.** One short table mapping each stated JD requirement to the question IDs that cover it, with `— none, and why` where a requirement earns no question. Checking the banks against the JD before delivering is what the rule above asks for and what does not happen; a table that has to be filled in is what makes an uncovered requirement visible to the writer as well as the reader. It is also the section a candidate scans first to decide whether the pack is worth their evening.
- **Session totals: roughly 18–26 questions.** Depth follows persona weight (4–5 / 3 / 2). Below ~18 the pack under-covers; above ~26 the candidate can't rehearse it all and the pack stops being prioritized advice.

## Provenance labels

Every question row carries one label, in its own Provenance column — not appended to the rationale, where it reads as punctuation and goes missing under exactly the conditions that matter most.

**Where the guidance coaches a claim about the candidate's own experience, the Provenance cell carries every sentence it rests on, each quoted verbatim, beside the label** — `[grounded] "Ran the small-grants round for three years"`. This is the fix that a rule could not deliver. Told in prose not to promote "ran" into "built" or "designed", packs did it anyway in every run of a fixture; asked to name the sentence the guidance stands on, the promotion has nowhere to hide, because the quote sits next to the claim and the gap between them is visible in one glance. A cell that cannot be filled is guidance resting on nothing, which is the finding, not an inconvenience. The three labels — `[grounded]`, `[inferred]`, `[generic]` — are defined in `references/evidence-conventions.md` §4, the suite's canonical vocabulary mirrored into this skill. Read the definitions there rather than from memory; the whole suite speaks one dialect, and a skill that quietly redefines a label breaks every consumer downstream of it.

Two connections that matter when writing a rationale cell:

- **Confidence and provenance are different axes.** Confidence (`evidence-conventions.md` §2) grades the *candidate's* evidence; provenance grades *what this pack wrote* on top of it. The mapping in `evidence-conventions.md` §5 says which is allowed from which — most usefully, a `low`-confidence claim may never be asserted as the candidate's story, only coached as a clarify-strategy note, and it is labeled `[inferred]` with the derivation named.
- **A `[generic]` label is an upgrade-menu argument, not an apology.** "These six `[generic]` rows become `[grounded]` if you add the JD text" tells the candidate exactly what their next five minutes of effort buys.

Why label at all: a candidate who rehearses a fabricated specific repeats it live and gets burned. The pack's honesty about its own epistemics is part of its quality.

## Writing the guidance cell

- **Speakable in 90–120 seconds.** Guidance is scaffolding for a spoken answer, not an essay. If it can't be delivered aloud in two minutes, cut it.
- **Story-mapped where a quoted sentence carries the story; a scaffold where none does — question by question, not pack by pack.** Where one of the candidate's sentences carries a story, name it and its beats — each beat a clause the quoted sentence states, any further beat an answer slot: `"ran the two-org dashboard rollout"` → the rollout (quoted) → where interests diverged: if they did, say how, in your own words; if not, say so and stay with the rollout. A beat structure written from the shape of a good story rather than from the sentence supplies the story's middle, which is the part an interviewer asks about. A dossier's `verified_candidate_evidence` is the strongest version of this: user-confirmed claims, mapped and labeled `[grounded]`, with any medium-confidence caveat coached into the answer rather than left for a prober to find. Where none does, give selection criteria instead: "pick a time you held a position under pressure from someone senior — it needs a named stake, a moment you didn't fold, and an intact relationship at the end." A CV with no sentence for this question is the scaffold case, not a licence to elaborate the nearest sentence it does have — the switch used to be whether a CV was present at all, and a pack holding one CV line per question stretched it to cover every question.
- **Metric questions include the weakest-assumption move.** Coach the candidate to name, in their own words, the assumption behind the figure they would defend least, before the interviewer does — it converts a gotcha into a credibility gain. The pack does not name the assumption unless a quote does; where none does, the move is an answer slot.
- **Pressure questions include the hold-then-concede structure.** Hold the position through two follow-ups, concede uncertainty explicitly and specifically, never fold on the core claim just to restore harmony. The position held is one the quoted material carries: a hold is where a pack most wants something stronger than the candidate said, and a held position they cannot back is the one the second follow-up breaks.
- **The rationale covers both inclusion and categorization.** Why this question is likely for this candidate in this loop, and why it carries these tags. A reader should be able to disagree intelligently with either call. It refers to the candidate's history only by fit-snapshot row ID, never restating it: once guidance had to quote, a rationale that paraphrased was the next cell an invented detail could sit in.
- **Truthful attribution throughout.** Guidance rehearses precise honesty — "what was actually you" — never spin. Coaching a candidate to inflate is coaching them to fail a probe that exists specifically to catch inflation.
- **Run the reframing audit over every guidance cell.** `references/reframing-audit.md`, the suite's canonical audit mirrored verbatim into this skill: six axes, five disciplines, clause by clause against the candidate's own sentences. A guidance cell is not advice about language, it *is* language — the candidate will say these words out loud to someone who was in the room. A story-mapped beat that quietly promotes "contributed to" into "led" hands them a sentence that collapses on the first follow-up, and it is the interviewer, not the pack, who finds out. The audit's own worked examples are drawn from resume lines; nothing about them is weaker here, and the stakes are higher, because a written claim can be walked back and a spoken one is already in the room. Two shapes this fails in, both found in clean-room runs. **Guidance that tells the candidate what their work is *proof of*** — "the small-grants round is proof you can run a pipeline other teams depend on" — is a claim about the work, not a rehearsal aid, and the candidate will repeat it as one. Coach them to describe what they did and let the interviewer draw the inference; an inference the candidate asserts on their own behalf is the one that gets tested. **And guidance that supplies specifics the candidate never gave** — a denominator, a timeline, a stakeholder's reaction — hands them a detail to say aloud that they cannot stand behind. Where the shape of a strong answer needs a specific, mark the slot: "name the metric here if you have one" is scaffolding; naming it for them is invention.

- **What any pack cell may say about the candidate's past, and the answer slot.** A clause that says what the candidate did, owned, decided or was responsible for rests on a quoted sentence, and says nothing that sentence does not say: it keeps the sentence's polarity, hedges, attribution and scope, so "never had direct reports" supports no claim about reports had, and "contributed to" never supports "led". Where the sentence must be depends on the surface. A question-bank cell rests on a sentence quoted in its own row's Provenance; a fit-snapshot cell on its own evidence cell; a cell with no Provenance — the values cheat sheet, a deliverable build, the interviewer's mirror opportunities — names the fit-snapshot row it draws on and rests on that row's evidence cell. Anywhere else in the evidence column is not a source, nor is a row reference, "as above", or a paraphrase, and the label does not change this. Every cell that speaks about the candidate counts — guidance, bridges, holds, rationale, those three, and the Question column — because a rule that binds one surface moves the claim to the next. What it stops is the drift that reads as faithfulness: a pack that knows the candidate "ran the grants programme" knows what running a grants programme usually involves, and writes those practices in as if the candidate had said them.
  A **Question cell** is the interviewer's line, not language coached for the candidate, and it may presuppose a practice, metric or responsibility only when a quoted source raises it — the JD, the recruiter's or interviewer's own words. It never supplies an event, an incident, a number or an outcome that neither the quotes nor those sources contain. Where a question presupposes something the row's quotes do not carry, the guidance holds an answer slot whose second branch allows the premise is false, and no hold, bridge or rationale adopts the premise as fact.
  An **answer slot** is the honest form, and the existing slot rule widened from a number to a practice, a method, a responsibility or an event. It names the practice only as the interviewer's likely probe, and only when a quoted source raises it — the JD, a fetched representative JD, the recruiter's or interviewer's own words; a `[generic]` requirement is never such a source. A figure the candidate gave raises one slot and no other: how it was counted, and which part was theirs. Then two branches: "if you did this, say how, in your own words; if there was no such step, or it was not yours, say so plainly and return to" a fact the quotes do carry. The second branch has to allow that the practice never existed, not only that someone else did it. An answer slot is an instruction for the interview, not a question for this conversation: it ends without a question mark, asks for no reply, and is not counted under `output-discipline.md` rule 1. Where the candidate's answer would make the pack better, say so in one upgrade-menu line naming the rows it would upgrade.
  The **presupposition test** decides what counts as an assertion: an instruction, a scripted line, a question, or "the same way you already X" that presupposes a practice, method, responsibility, decision, artifact, event or outcome the quotes do not state is asserting it — "walk them through your actual scoring rubric" claims there was a scoring rubric, and "if you chose which applications waited when the queue was full" claims there was a full queue. A hedge or a conditional elsewhere in the same cell does not cure it.
  A **plain acknowledgment** — a first-person negative, "I haven't managed a team" — is words the candidate will say, so it needs a source like any other: a negative the candidate stated, quoted in the evidence column or Provenance like any other quote, or a dossier `acknowledge_gap` area or `prohibited_claims` item, named by its field. A gap the material is merely silent on is not a negative the candidate stated. The pack may say what it observes — "your material does not show X" — in its own analysis, and the words for the candidate are an answer slot whose first branch they can answer truthfully.
  An answer slot never names a claim on this pack's prohibited list, whether the list came from a dossier or was built here: a slot that names a prohibited claim implies it. Those claims are coached only as the plain acknowledgment and its limit. On a dossier the same holds for an `acknowledge_gap` area. A `clarify` area takes the slot form `references/dossier-handoff.md` rule 2 spells out, and a `bridge` area keeps the underwriter's positioning and its named limit — the dossier's decision prose, `reasoning`, objections and conditions give the limit and how to position it, never a fact about the candidate's past, and every clause about what the candidate did still follows the rule above.

## Reverse questions

Every pack includes at least five, each with a one-line "why it lands." When an interviewer profile exists, at least one ties to their own career path — it converts an interrogation into a peer conversation and signals the candidate researched *them*, not just the company. Include one reciprocity question when the interviewer is a peer ("what could a new person do in their first month that would genuinely help your team?"). Never let the pack's answer to "any questions for me?" be generic — rapport-style interviewers weight this slot heavily as a preparation signal.
