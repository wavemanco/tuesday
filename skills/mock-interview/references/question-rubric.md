# Question types, coverage rules, and writing rules

## Contents

Personas describe how a question arrives; types describe what it's scoring. Tag every question with a type so the candidate can see the pack's coverage at a glance — and so you can see where coverage is thin before delivering.

## The five types

| Tag | Name | What it's really testing | Tends to dominate |
|---|---|---|---|
| **VAL** | Behavioral / values alignment | Whether past behavior demonstrates the company's *published* values, not generic virtue | Dedicated values rounds; cross-functional interviewers |
| **EXP** | Work-experience deep-dive | Whether the CV's claims survive scrutiny; attribution honesty; what the candidate actually did | Probers; anyone who read the CV closely |
| **JD** | Role / JD-driven | Stated responsibilities and qualifications — can the candidate do the listed job, including its gaps | Hiring managers; skeptics |
| **CO** | Company & strategy knowledge | Homework depth: strategy, competition, recent moves; whether the candidate can think at the company's altitude | Executives; strategy-adjacent roles |
| **CASE** | Hypothetical / live problem | Structure and reasoning on a problem from the actual role, delivered live | Hiring managers; case-led loops |

Dual-tagging is normal — list the primary type first and let the rationale explain the call ("JD content, CASE delivery"). A question with no clear type is usually a question that doesn't belong in the pack.

## Coverage rules

These exist because packs fail by omission, and the omissions are predictable:

- **Every hard requirement in the JD gets at least one JD-tagged question, and every other stated requirement gets one or a stated reason it does not.** The JD is the interviewer's scoring sheet; a requirement with no rehearsed answer is a free miss. The ones that go missing are never the headline requirements — they are the mid-list items, a named methodology or a specific tool, that appear once in the JD, get noted in the fit snapshot, and are never converted into a question. Check the finished banks against the JD's own list before delivering, not against your memory of it.
- **Every headline metric on the CV gets a defend-the-number EXP question.** Quantified claims are magnet targets for probers; the rehearsal is precise attribution, in the candidate's own words — how the figure was counted, what it is sensitive to, the assumption they would defend least, and exactly what was theirs. These four are the slots a figure raises (the answer-slot rule below). Where no quote states them, the guidance is an answer slot: the pack never supplies a sizing method, a baseline, an attribution model or an assumption, and "I don't know how it was counted" is an honest branch. A figure the candidate gave is exactly where a pack is tempted to explain how it was reached, and an explanation they cannot repeat under a second question is worse than none.
- **Every published company value gets at least one VAL question with a mapped story** when a values-style round is detected, as `references/research-checklist.md` defines it — mapped where one of the candidate's quoted sentences carries a story, and a selection scaffold ending in its honest branch (the open-prompt rule below) where none does. Slogan familiarity isn't the bar; a specific decision that demonstrates the value is.
- **At least one CO or CASE question grounded in the company's current strategy** when research at L1+ succeeded. These are the differentiator questions most candidates can't touch — and they go generic without research, which is the honest cost of declining the deep tier.
- **The single most predictable objection to this specific candidate gets the deepest rehearsal** — usually a Skeptic or Pressure item born from the fit snapshot's biggest gap. Name it in the pack as the highest-ROI prep, because it is: this question will be asked in some form, and a rehearsed non-defensive answer changes the interview. Deepest means the most follow-up levels, the most exact concession wording and a limit named before it is asked for — never more affirmative content than the candidate's quotes carry. The objection is usually aimed at exactly where the material is thin, which is where a pack is most tempted to thicken it.
- **Every pre-declared deliverable — a POV, presentation, or take-home the recruiter announced — gets its own build section.** Announced deliverables are graded deliverables: thesis options with a mandatory concession, a differentiation strategy, and the requested close (customer benefit, recommendation) if one was named. A pack that rehearses questions but not the assignment fails the loop.
- **When a provided research artifact is equally available to interviewers or rival candidates, differentiate from it — never recite it.** A public report's shortlist is the consensus answer any prepared candidate assembles; bring one consensus item to prove grounding, then one thesis-driven divergence, and where the role's real job includes saying no, the anti-pick. Reciting a shared source scores as homework; departing from it, with reasons, scores as judgment.
- **The pack carries a requirement-coverage line.** One short table mapping each stated JD requirement to the question IDs that cover it, with `— none, and why` where a requirement earns no question. Checking the banks against the JD before delivering is what the rule above asks for and what does not happen; a table that has to be filled in is what makes an uncovered requirement visible to the writer as well as the reader. It is also the section a candidate scans first to decide whether the pack is worth their evening.
- **Session totals: roughly 18–26 questions.** Depth follows persona weight (4–5 / 3 / 2). Below ~18 the pack under-covers; above ~26 the candidate can't rehearse it all and the pack stops being prioritized advice.

## Provenance labels

Every question row carries one label, in its own Provenance column — not appended to the rationale, where it reads as punctuation and goes missing under exactly the conditions that matter most.

**Where the guidance coaches a claim about the candidate's own experience, the Provenance cell carries every sentence it rests on, each quoted verbatim, beside the label** — `[grounded] "Ran the small-grants round for three years"`. This is the fix that a rule could not deliver. Told in prose not to promote "ran" into "built" or "designed", packs did it anyway in every run of a fixture; asked to name the sentence the guidance stands on, the promotion has nowhere to hide, because the quote sits next to the claim and the gap between them is visible in one glance. A cell that cannot be filled is guidance resting on nothing, which is the finding, not an inconvenience. The three labels — `[grounded]`, `[inferred]`, `[generic]` — are defined in `references/evidence-conventions.md` §4, the suite's canonical vocabulary mirrored into this skill. Read the definitions there rather than from memory; the whole suite speaks one dialect, and a skill that quietly redefines a label breaks every consumer downstream of it.

Two connections that matter when writing a rationale cell:

- **Confidence and provenance are different axes.** Confidence (`evidence-conventions.md` §2) grades the *candidate's* evidence; provenance grades *what this pack wrote* on top of it. The mapping in `evidence-conventions.md` §5 says which is allowed from which — most usefully, a `low`-confidence claim may never be asserted as the candidate's story, only coached as a clarify-strategy note, and it is labeled `[inferred]` with the derivation named.
- **A `[generic]` label is an upgrade-menu argument, not an apology.** "These six `[generic]` rows become `[grounded]` if you add the JD text" tells the candidate exactly what their next five minutes of effort buys.

Why label at all: a candidate who rehearses a fabricated specific repeats it live and gets burned. The pack's honesty about its own epistemics is part of its quality.

## Writing the guidance cell

- **Speakable in 90–120 seconds.** Guidance is scaffolding for a spoken answer, not an essay. If it can't be delivered aloud in two minutes, cut it.
- **Story-mapped where a quoted sentence carries the story; a scaffold where none does — question by question, not pack by pack.** Where one of the candidate's sentences carries a story, name it and its beats — each beat a clause the quoted sentence states, at its own attribution; a further beat that names a practice or an event is an answer slot, and only where a raising source raises it (the answer-slot rule below): `"Co-ran the joint funding call with the city council"` — CV, beside the JD's `"align priorities with partner funders"` → the joint call, co-run (quoted, so the share is the sentence's) → answer slot: the JD asks about aligning partner funders, so expect a probe on whether the funders' priorities differed on this call; if they did, say what happened and what part of it was yours, in your own words; if they did not, or nothing was done to align them, or none of it was yours, say so plainly and stay with the call. Where no raising source raises a further beat, the story has only the beats the sentence states, told in the candidate's own words. A beat structure written from the shape of a good story rather than from the sentence supplies the story's middle, which is the part an interviewer asks about. A dossier's `verified_candidate_evidence` is the strongest version of this: user-confirmed claims, mapped and labeled `[grounded]`, with any medium-confidence caveat coached into the answer rather than left for a prober to find. Where none does, give selection criteria instead, ending in the honest branch the open-prompt rule below requires: "pick a time you held a position under pressure from someone senior — it needs a named stake, a moment you didn't fold, and an intact relationship at the end; if no such time comes to mind, say so plainly, then give the nearest thing your material does carry, or how you would handle it." A CV with no sentence for this question is the scaffold case, not a licence to elaborate the nearest sentence it does have — the switch used to be whether a CV was present at all, and a pack holding one CV line per question stretched it to cover every question.
- **Metric questions include the weakest-assumption move.** Coach the candidate to name, in their own words, the assumption behind the figure they would defend least, before the interviewer does — it converts a gotcha into a credibility gain. The pack does not name the assumption unless a quote does; where none does, the move is the weakest-assumption slot a figure raises (the answer-slot rule below).
- **Pressure questions include the hold-then-concede structure.** Hold the position through two follow-ups, concede uncertainty explicitly and specifically, never fold on the core claim just to restore harmony. The position held is one the quoted material carries: a hold is where a pack most wants something stronger than the candidate said, and a held position they cannot back is the one the second follow-up breaks.
- **The rationale covers both inclusion and categorization.** Why this question is likely for this candidate in this loop, and why it carries these tags. A reader should be able to disagree intelligently with either call. It refers to the candidate's history only by fit-snapshot row ID, never restating it: once guidance had to quote, a rationale that paraphrased was the next cell an invented detail could sit in.
- **Truthful attribution throughout.** Guidance rehearses precise honesty — "what was actually you" — never spin. Coaching a candidate to inflate is coaching them to fail a probe that exists specifically to catch inflation.
- **Run the reframing audit over every guidance cell.** `references/reframing-audit.md`, the suite's canonical audit mirrored verbatim into this skill: six axes, five disciplines, clause by clause against the candidate's own sentences. A guidance cell is not advice about language, it *is* language — the candidate will say these words out loud to someone who was in the room. A story-mapped beat that quietly promotes "contributed to" into "led" hands them a sentence that collapses on the first follow-up, and it is the interviewer, not the pack, who finds out. The audit's own worked examples are drawn from resume lines; nothing about them is weaker here, and the stakes are higher, because a written claim can be walked back and a spoken one is already in the room. Two shapes this fails in, both found in clean-room runs. **Guidance that tells the candidate what their work is *proof of*** — "the small-grants round is proof you can run a pipeline other teams depend on" — is a claim about the work, not a rehearsal aid, and the candidate will repeat it as one. Coach them to describe what they did and let the interviewer draw the inference; an inference the candidate asserts on their own behalf is the one that gets tested. **And guidance that supplies specifics the candidate never gave** — a denominator, a timeline, a stakeholder's reaction — hands them a detail to say aloud that they cannot stand behind. Where the shape of a strong answer needs a specific, mark the slot: "name the metric here if you have one" is scaffolding; naming it for them is invention.

- **What any pack cell may say about the candidate's past, and the answer slot.** A clause that says what the candidate did, owned, decided or was responsible for rests on a quoted sentence, and says nothing that sentence does not say: it keeps the sentence's polarity, hedges, attribution and scope, so "never been involved in an audit" supports no claim about audit work, and "contributed to" never supports "led". Where the sentence must be depends on the surface. A question-bank cell rests on a sentence quoted in its own row's Provenance; a fit-snapshot cell on its own evidence cell; a cell with no Provenance — the values cheat sheet, a deliverable build, the interviewer's mirror opportunities — names the fit-snapshot row it draws on and rests on that row's evidence cell. Anywhere else in the evidence column is not a source, nor is a row reference, "as above", or a paraphrase, and the label does not change this. Every cell that speaks about the candidate counts — guidance, bridges, holds, rationale, those three, and the Question column — because a rule that binds one surface moves the claim to the next. What it stops is the drift that reads as faithfulness: a pack that knows the candidate "ran the grants programme" knows what running a grants programme usually involves, and writes those practices in as if the candidate had said them.
  **What a question may presuppose** is defined here, once; the template and SKILL.md point to this paragraph. A **raising source** is a quoted line from the supplied JD (a dossier's `role_profile` requirement text stands as the supplied JD's lines; its `unknowns` and inferred outcomes do not); from a fetched representative JD (that clause labelled `[inferred]` and framed as typical for the role, not as this team's practice); from the company's published values or principles (fetched or pasted, named in Sources); or from the recruiter's or interviewer's own words. A `[generic]` requirement, a search-result paraphrase and the skill's own role-family knowledge are never one. A Question cell is the interviewer's line, not language coached for the candidate, and a question about the candidate's past is one of two kinds. An **open prompt** — "Tell me about a time you…", "When have you…" — asks the candidate to choose an instance. It supplies no event, and its theme must come from a raising source or a persona stem in `references/personas.md`. Its guidance is a story map from a quoted sentence, or a selection scaffold that ends with an honest branch: "if no such time comes to mind, say so plainly, then give the nearest thing your material does carry, or how you would handle it". A **premised question** names or describes a particular occasion, artifact, number, method or outcome as the candidate's — "walk me through how you ran the round", "your scoring rubric", "the figure on your CV". It may presuppose only what a sentence quoted in its own row's Provenance says, keeping that sentence's scope, attribution and polarity. Where it presupposes a practice or responsibility that a raising source names and the row's quotes do not carry, its guidance is an answer slot whose second branch allows the premise is false — the practice never existed, not only that someone else did it. It never supplies an event, incident, number or outcome that neither its quotes nor a raising source contain, and never a negative without the source a plain acknowledgment needs (below). A dossier's `likely_interviewer_objections` item is asked as the underwriter wrote it only where every negative in it has that source; otherwise the unsourced negative is dropped from the Question, which asks about the requirement it was aimed at under the rules above, and the guidance follows that area's `allowed_interview_strategy` (`references/dossier-handoff.md` rule 2). No hold, bridge or rationale adopts a premise as fact. A live problem set in the role — a CASE question — asks about no past, and presupposes nothing about the candidate.
  An **answer slot** is the honest form, and the existing slot rule widened from a number to a practice, a method, a responsibility or an event. It names the practice only as the interviewer's likely probe, and only when a raising source raises it. A figure the candidate gave raises the defend-the-number slots of the coverage rule above, and only those: how it was counted, what it is sensitive to, the assumption behind it they would defend least, and which part was theirs. Each is asked in the candidate's own words; none names a method, model, baseline, process or responsibility, and a figure never raises a slot for any other practice. Then two branches: "if you did this, say how, in your own words; if there was no such step, or it was not yours, say so plainly and return to" a fact the quotes do carry. The second branch has to allow that the practice never existed, not only that someone else did it. An answer slot is an instruction for the interview, not a question for this conversation: it ends without a question mark, asks for no reply, and is not counted under `output-discipline.md` rule 1. Where the candidate's answer would make the pack better, say so in one upgrade-menu line naming the rows it would upgrade.
  The **presupposition test** decides what counts as an assertion: an instruction, a scripted line, a question, or "the same way you already X" that presupposes a practice, method, responsibility, decision, artifact, event or outcome the quotes do not state, or a negative without a plain acknowledgment's source, is asserting it; a question carries such a premise lawfully only as the open-prompt and premised-question rules above allow — "walk them through your actual scoring rubric" claims there was a scoring rubric, and "if you chose which applications waited when the queue was full" claims there was a full queue. A hedge or a conditional elsewhere in the same cell does not cure it.
  A **plain acknowledgment** — a first-person negative, "I haven't been involved in an audit" — is words the candidate will say, so it needs a source like any other: a negative the candidate stated — quoted in the evidence column or Provenance like any other quote, or recorded as their correction in a dossier `evidence_state.rejected_inferences` item, named by its field — or a dossier `acknowledge_gap` area or `prohibited_claims` item, named by its field. Named by its field means the cell that cites the item names that field anywhere in it, and quotes the item, if at all, whole and exactly as the dossier gives it — `prohibited_claims: "…"` is one form, not the only one; any other quotation in that cell is read as the candidate's words. A gap the material is merely silent on is not a negative the candidate stated. The pack may say what it observes — "your material does not show X" — in its own analysis, and the words for the candidate are an answer slot whose first branch they can answer truthfully.
  **The prohibited list** (section 13 of the template, built in Step 3 before the banks) holds two kinds of entry, each named with its basis, and each kind has one honest form. A **sourced entry** is a dossier `prohibited_claims` item, `acknowledge_gap` area or `evidence_state.rejected_inferences` item, a negative the candidate stated, or a claim one of their quotes contradicts. Its honest form is the quote that limits it — for a rejected inference, the sentence it was inferred from, at its own scope — plus the plain acknowledgment where the paragraph above gives one a source; a contradiction alone gets the contradicting quote and nothing more. An **inflated-form entry** is built locally: the stretched wording of a sentence the candidate did say. A requirement that widens a quoted sentence — the template's F1, "ran the round" against "own the pipeline" — is an inflated form and is listed; a requirement no quoted sentence speaks to is silence, is not listed, and is an answer slot. What an inflated-form entry lists is the wording, not a finding that the candidate lacks the thing, so its honest form is the quote at its own scope, with the limit said as the pack's own observation in analysis ("your material says the round, not the pipeline") and never as a first-person negative. Where a raising source asks about the wider claim, the slot stays inside the quote's scope — which of the round's stages were theirs to set — and never names the listed wording. No answer slot, hold, bridge or rationale asserts, softens or implies a claim on the list, whether the list came from a dossier or was built here, and a slot that names a listed claim implies it, even as a probe. In those cells only two things may name a listed claim: the pack's own observation of its limit, in the pack's analysis, and a sourced entry's plain acknowledgment where its source supports one; an inflated-form entry never gets a first-person negative. On a dossier the same holds for an `acknowledge_gap` area. A `clarify` area takes the slot form `references/dossier-handoff.md` rule 2 spells out, and a `bridge` area keeps the underwriter's positioning and its named limit — the dossier's decision prose, `reasoning`, objections and conditions give the limit and how to position it, never a fact about the candidate's past, and every clause about what the candidate did still follows the rule above.

## Reverse questions

Every pack includes at least five, each with a one-line "why it lands." When an interviewer profile exists, at least one ties to their own career path — it converts an interrogation into a peer conversation and signals the candidate researched *them*, not just the company. Include one reciprocity question when the interviewer is a peer ("what could a new person do in their first month that would genuinely help your team?"). Never let the pack's answer to "any questions for me?" be generic — rapport-style interviewers weight this slot heavily as a preparation signal.
