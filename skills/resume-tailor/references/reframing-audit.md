# The reframing audit

Canonical for every Tuesday skill that proposes language a candidate will say,
write, or send. Skills carry a mirror of this file in their `references/` with a
synced-from header; this copy wins on conflict.

It lives here rather than inside one skill because it is not one skill's
property. job-underwriter proposes resume lines and outreach phrasing;
resume-tailor's entire output is proposed language, row by row; any later skill
that drafts a sentence for a candidate inherits the same duty. Six axes and five
disciplines maintained in two places would diverge, in exactly the two places
they are load-bearing.

## The audit

**What it governs: every word a skill proposes the candidate say, write, or send.** Resume bullets and summary lines, yes — but equally the positioning advice ("lead with…", "your first two sentences should be…"), suggested outreach and cover-letter phrasing, and interview framing. Drift migrates to whichever surface the audit isn't watching, and prose advice is the easiest place for it to hide, because it reads as strategy rather than as a claim. It is still a claim: the candidate will say it out loud to someone who can check.

The baseline is what the user has offered as evidence about themselves — their
current resume line included. A draft of the text being written is a starting
point, not a baseline, and a line a skill proposed is never one. A
line with no predecessor, such as a summary written from nothing or a
cover-letter paragraph, is audited the same way: every clause of it traces to one
of the user's sentences, quoted, and a clause that traces to none is cut or asked
about.

Audit clause by clause against the user's own sentences, on six axes:

| Axis | The drift | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Scope** | Who or how many it touched grows | "our assessors use it" → "adopted foundation-wide" |
| **Verb** | Participation becomes origination or ownership | "chaired the review panel" → "founded the review panel" |
| **Kind** | The activity becomes a different, higher-status kind of work | "logged monitoring returns" → "evaluated grantee outcomes" |
| **Relationship** | Which side of the transaction the candidate stood on changes | assessed bids on the funder's side → "you know this fund from the applicant's side" |
| **Logical form** | A statement's logic weakens or inverts | "every bid was double-scored" → "bids were double-scored"; "all of X" → "some of X"; a non-negotiable → a preference |
| **Incident or practice** | An event, outcome, or stake the candidate never mentioned is supplied to make the story land — or an activity the role usually involves is attributed to them | never said any award was withdrawn → "you handled the withdrawal"; "ran the grants programme" → "set the scoring rubric and forecast the award budget" |

Notes on the harder ones. An invented **incident** and an entailed **practice** are the two that do not feel like inflation while you write them, because neither adds a credential. The incident supplies the drama a bullet seemed to be missing. The practice supplies what the role usually involves — whoever ran a grants programme presumably set its rubric — so it reads as a faithful reading of the stated fact rather than as an addition, and a writer who would never invent a title will write it without noticing. Both are facts the candidate will be asked about, and the honest version of each is a question: *did* anything fail, and were you there; *did* you set the rubric, was it set for you, or was there no rubric at all — the question leaves room for the practice never having existed, not only for someone else having done it. A label does not change this. `evidence-conventions.md` §4 lets `[inferred]` grade a reasonable extension of a grounded fact, and that covers a statement about the role ("programme officers usually own the rubric") or a question to the candidate — never a clause about the candidate's own past, because a practice they never stated has no evidence behind it, and `evidence-conventions.md` §5 lets evidence at `none` appear only as a named gap. **Logical form** is the easiest to write by accident, because the sentence still sounds like the candidate's: turning a conjunctive pair of non-negotiables ("I need X *and* Y") into a disjunctive one ("if *either* moves") reads as flexibility while quietly discarding half of what they told you they would not trade. On text the candidate actually sends, that costs them the outcome they set the constraint to protect. A change in **kind** is not a change in intensity — "collected" and "synthesized" describe different work, so one cannot stand in for the other. A **relationship** inversion is the subtlest of them and the most dangerous, because it usually arrives as a flattering insight ("you *are* the applicant this fund exists for") and collapses on the first follow-up question: a funder-side assessor and an applicant have different knowledge, different incentives, and different stories, and an interviewer on either side will know within a sentence which one they are talking to.

Five disciplines make the audit actually catch things:

- **Audit the whole line, every line.** A bullet whose ending was carefully checked while its opening verb drifted is the characteristic failure. So is a table audited scrupulously while the paragraph above it runs free.
- **Never borrow the JD's vocabulary for the candidate's own history.** She wrote "scored applications"; the posting said "investment decisions"; the proposed resume line said "made investment decisions". No fact changed and the words look like synonyms, which is exactly why the substitution survives review — but it turns a scorer whose marks went to a panel into the person who decided, in the employer's own preferred terms, and it does it inside a line she will say out loud. Mirror the JD's language in the analysis where it helps the reader follow the mapping; never in a sentence about the candidate's own past.
- **A number stays attached to the noun it came from.** "Monitoring visits across 40+ funded projects" counts projects, not visits; sliding the quantifier to the nearer noun manufactures a larger claim out of the candidate's own sentence, and it survives every check that looks only for invented figures, because the figure was not invented. Re-read the source clause before carrying any number into a proposal.
- **Ambiguity resolves downward.** When the candidate's own sentence admits two readings — "managed a £2m grants budget" is £2m a year or £2m across the whole post — take the reading that claims less, or ask. Resolving upward and then labelling the result `[grounded]` is how a fabricated credential acquires a citation.
- **Drift in the flattering direction is the one to hunt, but the rule is direction-agnostic** — untraceable language fails whether it raises or lowers the candidate.

**And when a clause cannot be traced to the user's own sentence, cut it or ask
about it.** That is not a sixth discipline; it is what the five are for. It was
counted as one from the start — the list said three, then four, then five with
it inside — and when a sixth discipline was added the lead-in stayed at five.
The restatements written after that already read the list as five disciplines
plus this rule, so moving it out made this file say what the other places repeating
"five disciplines" already said. An asked question can turn a
reframe into confirmed evidence; a guessed one turns it into a claim the
interview will test.

A self-certification is part of the audited text, not a substitute for it. Writing "every claim here traces to a sentence you wrote" above a line that does not is worse than writing nothing: it tells the candidate to stop checking. Make the claim only after the clause-by-clause pass, and if a clause cannot be traced, cut the clause rather than soften the certification.

Everything the audit governs is proposed, never applied — and the marker rides on **each** block, not once at the end. A resume-edits table, an outreach draft, an interview-framing script and a paragraph of positioning advice are four proposed artifacts; a single approval line beneath the table covers exactly one of them, and this rule has failed in that shape every time it has been measured. The table is the thing that *looks* like it needs approving, so it is the thing that gets marked while the prose beside it ships as advice. Mark each block where it stands — the edits table `resume_edits_pending_approval`, every other proposed artifact with its own `pending your approval` — because the candidate approves language, not tables.
